The problem

Today, the road is the test environment

To improve a junction, an engineer changes the timing on the real signal and watches real traffic. There is no rehearsal. If the change is wrong, the queue is real and the people sitting in it are real — and nothing records what was tried.

Adaptive traffic control already exists and works. Bengaluru runs B-ATCS on CoSiCoSt across roughly 165 junctions, with about a third less travel time reported at Hudson Circle; Pittsburgh's Surtrac cut travel time by a quarter. We are not competing with any of it. We built the thing that should sit next to it.

What it does

Preview, reason, receipt

Three things adaptive control does not give the person responsible for the junction.

PREVIEW

Try it before the street does

Drag a green longer and watch queues respond. Then test the plan against identical replayed traffic — same vehicles, same arrival times — so a better score is a better plan, not a luckier minute.

REASON

Every change says why

Automatic decisions carry one sentence citing the actual queue numbers, drawn beside the junction. Explainability is the documented number-one barrier to deploying learned control in real agencies.

RECEIPT

Priority has a price

Grant an emergency corridor and the ledger shows both sides: seconds saved for that vehicle, vehicle-seconds paid by cross traffic. Over budget, the request is refused — and it says which budget.

How it works

A junction is a scheduler

The four approaches are processes, green time is the CPU, a fixed plan is round robin, maximum green is starvation prevention, and an emergency corridor is preemption. Every controller — fixed, classical, or a language model — writes through one function, and the safety limits sit underneath it. That is why no model output can starve an approach or skip a yellow.

WhatHow it is modelled
Vehicle motionGap-based car following under finite acceleration — 40 km/h free flow, 1.5 m/s² acceleration, 3 m/s² braking, 2 m standstill gap, 2 s following headway
Traffic mixTwo-wheeler 0.25 PCU · auto-rickshaw 0.5 · car 1.0 · truck 2.2 · bus 3.0, each with its own length, speed and weight class
SignalsOpposing approaches as phase pairs, green → yellow → all-red, with minimum and maximum green enforced in the state machine itself
Start-up lost time2 seconds before a stopped queue moves — without it, capacity is overstated
Classical controlWebster's 1958 minimum-delay cycle from measured PCU flows, and Max-Pressure — the standard baseline in the research literature
MeasurementControl delay per vehicle, the measure used for level of service — not the smaller stopped-delay figure
RepeatabilityAll arrivals from one seeded generator, so two plans can be compared on identical traffic

Evidence

Same junction, same traffic, only the plan changed

A balanced plan gives about 16.7 seconds of average control delay per vehicle. Deliberately skew the same junction on the same seed and it becomes 21.0 seconds — the tool demonstrating its own premise.

Granting a priority corridor was measured too: the priority vehicle saved 2.9 seconds against the 6.3 seconds ordinary vehicles were losing in the same window, while cross traffic paid 334 vehicle-seconds. Under light traffic priority is a bad trade — and the tool says so rather than flattering itself.

What this does not do. No route choice, no turning movements, no pedestrians, no lane changing, and it has not been validated against field data. The sensor feed is mocked at one documented function. Every limitation is written down in the model specification rather than left to be discovered.
